# Evaluation for DD2352: Algorithms and Complexity (7.5 ECTS)

### Course Evaluation: DD2352 Algorithms and Complexity (7.5 ECTS)

#### 1. "Autodidact" Threshold
* **Verdict:** **PASS**
* **Rationale:** The course centers on rigorous theoretical concepts—formal computational complexity (P vs. NP, co-NP, reductions, NP-completeness proofs), amortized analysis, randomized algorithms, approximation algorithms, and undecidability. The material relies heavily on formal mathematical proofs, formal reductions, and pen-and-paper analysis that require structured feedback and are notoriously difficult to self-teach compared to application- or API-driven subjects.

#### 2. Transferability
* **Verdict:** **High**
* **Rationale:** Computational complexity and algorithmic paradigms (dynamic programming, greedy strategies, network flows, approximation, randomized methods) are fundamental mental models applicable across software engineering, quantitative finance, operations research, machine learning system optimization, and distributed systems.

#### 3. Prerequisite Knowledge
* **Verdict:** **High**
* **Rationale:** The course establishes a foundational theoretical framework ("map") necessary to navigate advanced computer science domains (cryptography, quantum computing, algorithmic game theory, advanced optimization), while the theoretical skill ceiling remains exceptionally high with vast ongoing academic research and deep sub-fields.

#### 4. Frequently Used on the Job
* **Verdict:** **Moderate**
* **Rationale:** While standard software engineers rarely construct formal NP-completeness proofs or prove lower bounds daily, the ability to identify intractable problems, evaluate asymptotic bottlenecks, and select appropriate algorithmic trade-offs (e.g., heuristics vs. exact solutions) is regularly relied upon in technical system design, research, and performance-critical engineering.

---

### Strategic Takeaway
DD2352 serves as a high-leverage theoretical foundation that instills durable problem-solving and tractability-analysis skills across computing domains, despite the direct day-to-day work leaning more heavily on algorithmic intuition than formal mathematical proofs.
